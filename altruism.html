Welfarism

We seek to be altruistic—selfless, the contra to ego—yet the means by which we do this are not grounded upon these “fundamental axioms.” Standing upon Singer’s utilitarian framework of complete moral impartiality, we happily stomp on cockroaches and electrocute shrimp en masse (we consider this preferable). Wild existence is predatory and wholly suffering (vegetarian diets do not prevent this, and turning everybody vegan is not feasible); thus, is the moral resolution of feral beings death? Where are we to define the lower bound of expected living welfare at which we seek to preserve life? The lion will eat the zebra inherently, as will a Kiwi eat lamb. Most of us seem to have chosen the most destitute of our fellow humans as our lower bound. Why, then, do we believe that human welfare is an appropriate objective function? If we make a comparison with the animal kingdom (assuming we are disjoint), a key discrepancy is that we, for the most part, do not kill each other for food, can communicate articulately, and are self-reliant, etc. This is intelligence.

Intelligence is the determinant from which we derive our empathy, such that I do not kill a pigeon when I am hungry. We have the capacity for hypotheticals (I would not want to be the pigeon). This perspective prevents me from falling into human-centric moral descriptivism; concrete morality is not a prerequisite for sentience, and we ourselves do not possess it. We can be somewhat impartial; unlike us, the animal is absolutely hylic. Do we interfere in animal suffering or allow the irrational, physical universe to proceed? Our partial intelligence cannot license the total administration of the physical without risking our becoming another demiurge. We are therefore responsible first for the hells we manufacture.
